My husband and I recently spent several weeks walking across the English Lake District and Scottish Highlands. In the Lake District, we walked the 78-mile Cumbria Way, and in Scotland, the almost 100-mile West Highland Way . We filmed the journey along the way, and have just finished putting the two films together.


The West Highland Way & Isle of Skye

The Cumbria Way

But the films aren't really what I want to write about here. I've been thinking about everything that happened before we left: the months of planning, preparation and anticipation, and what happened when the plan eventually met reality.

In my day job, I work on building software that enables teams to plan and track their work better, make stronger decisions, and turn ideas swiftly into something they can execute. Naturally, I spend a lot of time at work thinking about what it means to plan well.

Outside work, too, I am an inveterate planner. Every time we go on vacation, I take a lot of joy in planning our days. I watch videos online, purchase guidebooks, and devour anything that will help me have a more predictable experience when I am actually out there. I like knowing what there is to see, what the options are, how long something might take, where we might eat, and what we might encounter along the way. This predilection is especially keen when I am planning our long-distance hiking trips, where there is something comforting about having some certainty amidst everything else that can be uncertain about the day.


The Preparation

This journey was, in many ways, an example of my compulsive planning. I created a document that had everything listed day by day. What was the source town and the destination town? What hotel would we sleep in? What were the mileage and elevation gain and loss for the day? What were the famous photography locations in that stage? Where were the places we might want to stop for food? Was it perchance a new moon, and could I hope for astrophotography? There was deep satisfaction in taking a long, complicated trip that existed only as a vague idea in my head and slowly turning it into a sequence of concrete days that I could imagine myself walking through.

We trained and prepared for this trip for months. The first entry into the planning document was made on April 5th. We departed for the UK on August 23rd. In the intervening months, I booked hotels, reworked the schedule when hotels were already sold out, which happened more than once in the remote, rural sections of both walks, bought missing gear, lifted heavier weights to get stronger, walked longer distances over the weekend, and fine-tuned our packing list.

I also built up a whole second set of dreams around the trip. I imagined the footage I wanted to capture on the trails. I learned more about sound engineering and color grading and how to put together a compelling travel film. I thought about the shots I wanted, the stories we could tell, and how I might put the clips together when we got home. In a way, I had already started experiencing the trip months before we ever left. My anticipation and excitement were building alongside the preparation.

I am pretty sure all of this planning and training and preparation had a lot to do with us ultimately having such a good time on the West Highland Way and the Cumbria Way. Without the preparation, we would not have known that we needed a strategy for combating midges on the trail ( smidge AND midge nets were essential). We might not have understood how important it was to book hotels in advance in remote locations like Kingshouse . Without getting my body used to walking 15-plus miles, we may not have been able to complete some of the longest and most demanding stretches of the trail. And without preparing our minds to accept the reality of unceasing rain, partly by deliberately training in unceasing rain, we might have thrown in the towel when faced with inexorably dreary weather.


The Reality

When the actual moment came, we accepted what nature threw at us. There was rain, wind, mud, cold, blisters, wet boots, and days that were harder than we had anticipated. The days were not as picture perfect as I'd imagined they would be when I had planned my shot list. But accepting reality doesn't mean accepting that there is nothing you can do about it. We still had room to pivot.

For example, we wanted to see the signature Rannoch Moor on the West Highland Way on a good weather day. The day we were supposed to walk it, according to our carefully constructed plan, was going to be rainy and windy throughout. In foul weather, the moor can be inhospitable, with very little shelter or refuge. So we decided we would change the plan. We swapped two stages of the hike and used public transport to get us to the start and the end, while still walking the whole distance, just in a different order than what had been planned. Nothing about the goal had changed: we would walk every mile of the Cumbria Way and the West Highland Way. We simply changed the path we took to get there.

I think this is true in work and careers too.

Preparation isn't about eliminating uncertainty. It isn’t about having an infallible plan you never deviate from. It is about creating enough context and enough options that you can make good decisions when uncertainty arrives. The best plans I've seen aren't necessarily the ones that survive contact with reality unchanged. They're the ones that help people recognize what needs to change and decide what to do next. A plan gives you something to work from, but it shouldn't become something you feel compelled to defend when the circumstances that produced it have changed.

And perhaps that's what I liked most about walking the West Highland Way and the Cumbria Way. We planned carefully. We trained. We carried the gear we thought we'd need. We spent months imagining the experience before we ever set foot on the trails. And then we learned to let the day tell us what the day was going to be.

Sometimes that meant changing the route. Sometimes changing the schedule. Sometimes just accepting that we were going to get wet and soldiering on. Sometimes it meant acknowledging that we were more tired than we had expected and giving ourselves permission to slow down or start late. And sometimes there wasn't anything particularly clever to do at all. The weather was the weather, the trail was the trail, and the only thing left was to keep walking.

There is almost always something you can do. And when there isn't, there is still a choice about how you respond to what is in front of you. I think that is one of the less obvious benefits of preparation. It doesn't give you control over the future. It gives you more ways to respond to it.

We cannot control everything that happens. But we have agency over what is ours to shape: how we prepare, how we respond, when we change course, and when we let go. Perhaps real intelligence is not in trying to control every variable, but in recognizing which ones we can influence, acting decisively on those, and embracing the reality of the ones we cannot.
